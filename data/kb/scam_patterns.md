# Scam pattern knowledge base

Written in our own words from public consumer guidance (FTC consumer.ftc.gov, FBI IC3 ic3.gov, and university career-services scam advisories). Each "##" section is one retrievable chunk.

## Fake job offer – equipment purchase / fake check
How it works: A "recruiter" offers a remote job (often data entry, virtual assistant, or customer service) after little or no interview. They mail or email a check and ask you to deposit it, buy "equipment" or "software" from their approved vendor, and send the remaining money back. The check later bounces and you lose the money you sent.
Red flags: hired without a real interview; interview over Telegram, WhatsApp, Signal, or text; check arrives before you start; vendor payment by Zelle, gift cards, or crypto.
Verify safely: Look up the company's careers page yourself and confirm the posting exists. Contact the company's HR through the phone number on its official website. Never deposit a check from an employer you haven't verified, and never send money back.

## Fake job offer – pay to get hired
How it works: The offer is real-looking but requires a fee for training, certification, a background check, visa processing, or a "refundable" deposit.
Red flags: any upfront payment; pressure to pay quickly; payment to a personal account or app.
Verify safely: Legitimate employers pay for their own background checks and training. Ask your university career center to check the employer. Search the company name plus "scam".

## Recruiter impersonation of a real company
How it works: Scammers copy a real company's logo and name (for example a large tech or consulting firm) but write from a free email account (gmail, outlook) or a lookalike domain such as company-careers.net or company-hr.com.
Red flags: email domain doesn't match the company's official website; offer letter with spelling errors or generic signatures; requests for SSN, bank details, or passport copies before formal onboarding.
Verify safely: Compare the sender's domain to the domain of the company's official website. Apply only through the company's official careers portal. Call the company's main switchboard to confirm the recruiter exists.

## Task or "commission" job scam
How it works: You're paid small amounts to "rate products", "boost apps", or "complete tasks", then told to deposit your own money (often crypto) to unlock higher-paying tasks or withdraw earnings.
Red flags: contact out of the blue on WhatsApp or Telegram; earnings shown in an app you can't withdraw from; requests to top up with crypto.
Verify safely: Real jobs never require you to pay to earn. Stop and don't send more money.

## Credential phishing – account suspended / verify identity
How it works: An email or text claims your bank, email, Microsoft 365, university, or streaming account is locked, suspended, or showing unusual activity, and links to a fake login page.
Red flags: urgency ("within 24 hours"); link domain doesn't match the real company; generic greeting; sender display name doesn't match the address; failed SPF/DKIM/DMARC.
Verify safely: Don't click the link. Open the app or type the official website address yourself and check for alerts there. Report phishing to your IT department or to reportphishing@apwg.org.

## Package delivery scam (smishing)
How it works: A text says a USPS, FedEx, UPS, or DHL package is on hold because of an address problem or unpaid fee, with a link to "reschedule".
Red flags: you weren't expecting a package; tiny fee requested; link uses a shortened URL or a domain that isn't the carrier's official site.
Verify safely: Track packages only through the carrier's official website or app using a tracking number from the retailer. USPS does not text links asking for payment unless you signed up with a tracking number.

## Gift card / boss impersonation (business email compromise)
How it works: A message appears to come from your manager, professor, CEO, or a department head asking for an urgent favor: buying gift cards, changing payroll direct deposit, or paying an invoice.
Red flags: "I'm in a meeting, can't talk"; secrecy; gift cards; a new bank account for an existing vendor; reply-to address different from the sender.
Verify safely: Confirm through a separate channel you already trust, such as calling the person on a known number or speaking in person. Never buy gift cards for payment requests.

## Government / tax impersonation
How it works: A caller, email, or text claims to be the IRS, Social Security Administration, immigration, or police, threatening arrest, deportation, or fines unless you pay immediately.
Red flags: threats; payment by gift card, wire, or crypto; demands to stay on the phone; claims your SSN is suspended.
Verify safely: Government agencies usually contact you by mail first and never demand gift cards or crypto. Call the agency using the number on its official .gov website.

## Prize, lottery, refund, or inheritance scam
How it works: You're told you won a prize, are owed a refund, or inherited money, but must pay a fee or share bank details to receive it.
Red flags: you didn't enter a contest; fee to claim; request for bank login or account numbers.
Verify safely: Real prizes don't require payment. Look up the organization independently and contact it directly.

## Account takeover via one-time code
How it works: Someone pretending to be a friend, a marketplace buyer, or support staff asks you to send them a verification code you just received by text.
Red flags: any request to share a one-time code or "verification code"; claims they sent it to you by mistake.
Verify safely: Never share one-time codes. That code is the key to your account. If a friend asks, call them directly to confirm.

## Crypto investment / romance scam
How it works: A new online contact builds trust over weeks, then introduces a "guaranteed" crypto or trading platform. Early withdrawals work; later you can't withdraw and are asked to pay "taxes" or "fees".
Red flags: guaranteed high returns; contact you've never met in person; pressure to invest more.
Verify safely: Check the platform with your country's financial regulator (e.g. SEC or FINRA BrokerCheck in the U.S.). Talk to someone you trust before sending money.

## Signs of a legitimate communication (still verify)
Common traits of genuine messages: sender domain matches the organization's official website; SPF, DKIM and DMARC pass; links point to the organization's own domain; no request for passwords, codes, gift cards, or fees; recruiting goes through an applicant portal (Workday, Greenhouse, Lever) after real interviews with identifiable people; offer letters reference I-9 or E-Verify and are sent from the company domain.
Even when all of these hold, verify important requests (money, credentials, personal documents) through a channel you look up yourself.
